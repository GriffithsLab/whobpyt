Design philosophy#

WhoBPyT comes out of the research programme of the Whole-Brain Modelling Group: building patient-personalized brain simulations that help interpret neuroimaging and neurostimulation data. Neuroscience is very good at measuring brain activity and at perturbing it, for example with TMS or intracranial stimulation. It is less good at explaining why the measurements look the way they do, and what actually happens in the brain when we stimulate it. Whole-brain models are a way of answering those questions mechanistically, in silico. The principles below follow from that goal.

Models are fitted to individual people#

The aim is not a generic simulation of “a brain”, but a model of this person’s brain, fitted to their EEG, MEG or fMRI recordings. Each model is built on a subject’s (or a template) connectome and, for EEG/MEG, on a lead field that maps brain regions to sensors. Fitting it to their data yields subject-specific parameter estimates that can then be compared across people, conditions, ages, or treatment outcomes.

Fitting by gradient descent through the simulation#

Estimating physiological parameters by fitting whole-brain models to data is hard: networks are large, the equations are detailed and fast, and simulations need to be long. WhoBPyT tackles this with the optimization tools of deep learning. Its models are written in PyTorch as if they were deep neural networks, so automatic differentiation gives the gradient of a loss comparing simulated and recorded data with respect to every learnable parameter, and industry-standard optimizers update them all at once. This approach was introduced in Griffiths et al. (2022) and is described in detail on the Algorithms and other implementation details page.

Parameters stay physiologically meaningful#

Although the fitting machinery comes from deep learning, the models are not black boxes. Every parameter is a quantity with a biophysical meaning, such as a synaptic time constant, a connection strength or a conduction velocity. Each one can be fixed or learnable and can carry a prior that keeps it in a plausible range. That is what lets a fitted model say something about mechanism. For example, in Momi et al. (2023), subject-specific estimates pointed to inhibitory (GABAergic) populations as a key factor in scaling cortical excitability.

Models are tools for testing hypotheses#

Once a model reproduces someone’s data, it can be manipulated in ways the real brain cannot. Connections can be cut at precise moments (“virtual lesions”), parameters can be changed one at a time, and stimulation can be delivered anywhere. The paper replications in the examples gallery use this to ask, for instance, which parts of a TMS-evoked response depend on recurrent feedback from the rest of the network.

Building blocks are interchangeable#

A fitting problem combines a model, a set of parameters, an objective function and a fitting procedure. WhoBPyT keeps these separate, with abstract base classes defining how they fit together, so that each can be swapped or extended independently. See Code architecture for details.

Check what the fitted model captures#

A good fit is the start of an analysis, not the end. Fitted parameters should be checked for how much individual-level information they actually carry, and for how robust they are. Because the PyTorch implementations may differ in small ways from the original form of a model, it is good practice to confirm that fitted parameters reproduce the same behaviour in an independent implementation.

Open and reproducible#

Whole-brain modelling should be reproducible and accessible to people outside the groups that develop it. WhoBPyT is open source, its example datasets are publicly hosted, and each published study that uses it comes with a runnable, step-by-step replication in the examples gallery. Tutorials and courses, such as the OHBM brain modelling course, build on the same materials.
